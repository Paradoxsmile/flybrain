\begin{abstract}

New connectome reconstructions are typed by matching their neurons to an annotated reference. Most cell types are rare: three quarters of the 8,772 types of the FlyWire brain have two to four neurons, and connectivity-based typing needs labeled partners in the target. We type rare neurons without any target label in two steps. A graph neural network that sees only anonymous wiring, transferred from the reference, selects the candidate neurons, and graph matching against the reference's rare-type neurons types them. We tested the method under protocols committed before any run on held-out data. On the sparse BANC brain it raised rare-type accuracy from 22 to 41~\%; from the male nerve cord MANC to the nerve cords of BANC and of the male CNS, from 40 to 61~\% and from 72 to 82~\%. References built from morphology or from curated typing gave the same gains. Wiring complements morphology: on BANC's brain, automatic morphology matches (NBLAST) alone type 67~\% of the rare-type neurons, and taking the matched type wherever graph matching assigns a neuron raises this to 73~\% (exploratory). The same tests mark the limits. A model-selection rule failed on a sparse reconstruction, a fallback tuned on the brain failed in the nerve cord, and a partial volume, the hemibrain, cannot be typed from wiring. For common types, the transferred GNN types a second complete brain at 93~\%, mostly in the optic lobe, but a few percent of hand labels propagated by connectivity do better. The approach is most useful for the rare types that a labeling budget reaches last.

\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Electron-microscopy connectomes of the adult \emph{Drosophila} central nervous system now exist for several individuals: the female FAFB/FlyWire brain (\citealp{dorkenwald2024}; \citealp{schlegel2024}), the hemibrain (\citealp{scheffer2020}), the male ventral nerve cord MANC (\citealp{takemura2024}; \citealp{marin2024}), the male CNS (MCNS; \citealp{berg2026}) and the female brain and nerve cord BANC (\citealp{bates2026}). Their value rests on cell-type annotations. New reconstructions are typed by matching their neurons to an annotated reference, by morphology and by connectivity (\citealp{schlegel2024}; \citealp{bates2026}). Most of the types are small: of FlyWire's 8,772 cell types, about three quarters have two to four neurons. Connectivity-based typing compares each neuron's synaptic partners by type, as in the definition of optic-lobe types (\citealp{matsliah2024}) and in NTAC (\citealp{schwartzman2026}), and therefore needs labeled partners in the target.

We study the complementary setting: methods that see only the anonymous wiring diagram, the directed graph of neurons and synapse counts, applied to a target in which no neuron is labeled. For common types, a trained GNN can be transferred between connectomes. For rare types, a trained classifier has seen one or two examples per type. These types were defined by matching neurons across hemispheres and brains, and pairing neurons is what graph matching does without labels (\citealp{pedigo2023}). We therefore let the transferred GNN decide which target neurons to match and graph matching decide their types. Our contributions:

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*,itemsep=2pt]

\item \textbf{Label-free typing of rare types by a GNN and graph matching} (Figure~\ref{fig:1}). Within FlyWire, graph matching types the right-hemisphere neurons of 2–4-neuron types at 92~\%, against 76~\% for the GNN; when the GNN selects the candidates, the label-free combination reaches 86~\%. Between FlyWire and the male CNS it raises these types from 54 to 75~\%, and to 82~\% with improved candidates (Sections 3.3, 4.3).

\item \textbf{Pre-registered tests, failures included.} On BANC's brain (22 → 41~\%) and from MANC to the nerve cords of BANC (40 → 61~\%) and MCNS (72 → 82~\%), the gain held, also against references built from morphology or curated typing; candidate selection is the bottleneck (Section 4.4). The tests also showed that the original selection rule fails on sparse reconstructions, that density explains about half of the loss on BANC, that a fallback tuned on the brain fails in the nerve cord, and that partial volumes are beyond the approach (Sections 4.4, 4.5).

\item \textbf{Wiring adds to morphology.} On BANC's rare types, unreviewed NBLAST matches type 66.5~\% of the brain neurons and wiring alone 51.2~\%; the matched type where graph matching assigns a neuron and NBLAST's elsewhere reaches 72.5~\% (+6.0 points, 95~\% interval 5.1–7.1). In the nerve cord, where raw NBLAST is weak (30.5~\%), the combination reaches 64.9~\%. These comparisons are exploratory (Section 4.4).

\item \textbf{A wiring-only GNN, label-free transfer, and what it is worth.} A Dir-SAGE GNN (\citealp{rossi2023}) with synapse-weighted means types 434 FlyWire cell types at 95.3~\% (NTAC with labeled partners: 97.0~\%) and keeps 87–95~\% on an unlabeled hemisphere, where NTAC falls to 8–9~\% (Section 4.1). Prior matching of type sizes and a label-free choice of preprocessing transfer it to the male CNS at 92.6~\%, carried by the optic lobe (Section 4.2). On complete targets it is worth about 1.3~\% hand labels for common types, more for small ones, and seed agreement points reviewers to most errors (Section 4.6).

\end{enumerate}

We report the failures with the same care as the successes. Each of the four pre-registered tests revealed something the development data had hidden.

\section{Related work}

\textbf{Cell typing from connectomes.} Fly connectomes have been annotated with thousands of types from morphology and connectivity (\citealp{scheffer2020}; \citealp{schlegel2024}; \citealp{matsliah2024}; \citealp{marin2024}). In the optic lobe, types were defined from each neuron's input and output vectors over types (\citealp{matsliah2024}). NTAC (\citealp{schwartzman2026}) automates this from labeled seeds (97.0~\% on 434 FlyWire types in our reimplementation, Section 4.1) and has an unsupervised mode. Learned approaches mostly use morphology (\citealp{troidl2024}) or combine it with connectivity (NeuNet; \citealp{liao2024}), where connectivity alone reached 59~\% on hemibrain classes. Across datasets, types are matched by NBLAST, connectivity alignment and combinations of both (\citealp{schlegel2024}; \citealp{bates2026}; \citealp{berg2026}). The typing methods read partner labels or are evaluated within one labeled graph; we know of no evaluation of a trained typing model transferred to an unlabeled connectome.

\textbf{Graph matching and label-free structure.} Graph matching finds the correspondence between the nodes of two graphs that best aligns their edges; the relaxed quadratic assignment problem is solved efficiently by Frank–Wolfe iterations (FAQ; \citealp{vogelstein2015}). Bisected graph matching, which also aligns the connections between hemispheres, pairs bilaterally homologous neurons of the larval connectome from connectivity alone (\citealp{pedigo2023}). Adjacency spectral embedding with Gaussian mixtures recovers neuron groups in the larval mushroom body without labels (\citealp{priebe2017}). These methods match or cluster neuron by neuron; we use graph matching as the second stage after a transferred classifier has selected the candidates, across animals and connectomes.

\textbf{Directed and heterophilic graph learning.} Neurons mostly connect to neurons of other types, a heterophilic setting in which standard message passing struggles (\citealp{zhu2020}). Aggregating separately over in- and out-neighbors (Dir-GNN; \citealp{rossi2023}) raises effective homophily; our model is Dir-SAGE (\citealp{hamilton2017}; \citealp{rossi2023}) with synapse-weighted means.

\textbf{Domain adaptation without target labels.} Prior matching is a form of label-shift correction (\citealp{saerens2002}) and of optimal transport with fixed class marginals (Sinkhorn scaling), and it mirrors reference mapping in single-cell genomics, where batch effects separate a query from an annotated atlas. Our revised selection rule uses agreement between independently trained models, which tracks their error under distribution shift (\citealp{jiang2022}).

\subsection{Model}

Each neuron has six features: in- and out-degree, input and output synapses, and mean synapses per input and output connection (log-scaled, z-scored). A layer computes $h'_i = W_{\mathrm{self}}\, h_i + W_{\mathrm{in}}\, \mathrm{mean}_{\mathrm{in}}(h) + W_{\mathrm{out}}\, \mathrm{mean}_{\mathrm{out}}(h)$, with means over pre- and postsynaptic partners weighted by synapse count. Cell types use 3 layers (300 epochs, class-weighted loss), super classes 4 layers (1,000 epochs); depth and options were chosen by validation macro-F1. With thousands of types the output layer and prior matching are computed in chunks, which reproduces the standard code where both can run. The model never sees a partner's label, so it applies unchanged to an unlabeled graph.

Baselines, ordered by the information they use: majority class; logistic regression on a neuron's own features; label propagation; a partner profile (logistic regression on the share of synapses per partner type, using training labels); NTAC (\citealp{schwartzman2026}; a memory-lean reimplementation checked step by step against the published package); GraphSAGE (inputs only, unweighted). Splits: random (70/15/15, stratified) and hemisphere (train and validate on one hemisphere, test on the other). Three seeds throughout.

\subsection{Label-free transfer}

A model trained on the source is applied to the target. Every choice is made without target labels:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,itemsep=2pt]

\item \textbf{Candidates.} Each target is built in a few alignment variants (Section 3.4); target features are normalized either with the source's training statistics or with the target's own; prior matching is applied with strength $\rho \in \{0, 0.25, 0.5, 0.75, 1\}$.

\item \textbf{Prior matching.} Cell-type sizes are stereotyped across flies, but a transferred classifier shifts neurons between neighboring types. We rescale its class probabilities with weights v (Sinkhorn iterations) so that the expected number of neurons per type matches the type distribution the same model predicts on its source, scaled to the target; ρ interpolates between the raw prediction and full matching ($p_k \cdot v_k^{\rho}$, renormalized).

\item \textbf{Selection.} Originally, the candidate whose labeling (mean over three seeds) is most self-consistent on the target: each neuron of a random half gets the label of its most similar neuron in the other half (synapses per partner type, weighted Jaccard), and agreement is chance-corrected (Cohen's κ). The revised rule (Section 4.5), used in all later tests, takes the best mean rank of two other label-free properties: the Jensen-Shannon divergence of the predicted type sizes from the prior, and the agreement between the three seeds' labelings.

\item \textbf{Refinement.} NTAC on the target, seeded with the neurons on which all three seeds agree.

\end{itemize}

\subsection{Two-stage typing of rare types}

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,itemsep=2pt]

\item \textbf{Stage 1} is the label-free transfer of Section 3.2 at the ≥ 2 label set.

\item \textbf{Candidates} are the target neurons that stage 1 assigns to a rare type (2–4 source neurons). In the first tests, the type after NTAC decides (\texttt{gnn}). After the BANC rare-type test, candidate selection was improved on development data (Section 4.4); the improved rule, \texttt{union}, takes a neuron if its type before or after NTAC is rare.

\item \textbf{Stage 2} matches the source's rare-type neurons one-to-one to the candidates. It maximizes the agreement of the two log1p(synapse) adjacency matrices among the selected neurons by Frank–Wolfe on the relaxed quadratic assignment problem (FAQ; \citealp{vogelstein2015}), from the barycenter, for 30 iterations. The assignment is block-diagonal by hemisphere and aligns both ipsilateral and contralateral connections (bisected matching; \citealp{pedigo2023}). In the nerve cord a third, pooled block holds the neurons that pass through the neck (ascending and descending), whose side annotation follows the neck in MANC and the soma in the targets. Each block is padded to a square; candidates matched to padding are not assigned.

\item \textbf{Final labeling.} A matched candidate takes the type of its source neuron; every other neuron keeps its stage-1 type.

\item \textbf{Diagnostic.} With \textbf{oracle candidates} (the labeled rare-type target neurons), the matching shows what perfect candidate selection would give.

\end{itemize}

At the barycenter the gradient is nearly of rank one and the exact assignment solver takes 5–10 minutes, so the first step pairs the leading singular vectors by rank. A block of about 9,300 neurons per side takes 27 minutes and 4.5 GB on a CPU, the nerve-cord blocks under 3 minutes.

\subsection{Alignment variants}

Connectomes differ in synapse detection and completeness. Each target gets variants that align one statistic with the source by a fixed rule: \texttt{raw} (threshold 5, nothing aligned), \texttt{density} (the threshold whose connections per neuron are closest to the source's) and \texttt{scaled} (threshold 5, counts scaled so that synapses per neuron match the source's). For MCNS against FAFB we use Janelia's published scaling factor (0.581) for the last rule. In the nerve-cord test, self-connections and connections between two passing neurons, which in the targets can lie in the brain, are dropped in all three graphs.

\subsection{Pre-registration and development sandbox}

For each test on a new dataset, data, labels, candidates, selection rule, method, the reported numbers and explicit expectations were committed to the repository before any model was run on it: \texttt{docs/\allowbreak{}banc\_\allowbreak{}protocol.md} (BANC brain, common types), \texttt{docs/\allowbreak{}hemibrain\_\allowbreak{}protocol.md}, \texttt{docs/\allowbreak{}banc\_\allowbreak{}rare\_\allowbreak{}protocol.md} (BANC brain, rare types) and \texttt{docs/\allowbreak{}vnc\_\allowbreak{}rare\_\allowbreak{}protocol.md} (nerve cords). The protocols allow only data-loading and memory fixes afterwards and require every deviation to be reported. Between tests, methods were changed only on a sandbox built from the development connectomes, with known labels: synapse counts binomially thinned before the threshold, cell types removed, the volume restricted to the central brain, or a fraction of neurons cropped (each synapse of a cropped neuron kept with probability 0.1). For partial volumes, a coverage prior uses the type distribution predicted for the FAFB neurons inside the covered volume, those with at least 10~\% of their synapses outside the optic-lobe neuropils; it needs only the list of covered neuropils.

With labeled partners, NTAC is the best cell-type method; the GNN comes close (95.3 vs. 97.0~\%, equal macro-F1) without seeing any partner label and is best on super classes. On an unlabeled hemisphere, the median test neuron has no synapse to a labeled partner (optic-lobe circuits stay on their side), and partner-label methods collapse on cell types (1–9~\%; NTAC stays below 9.4~\% at any of 40 steps), while the GNN keeps 87–95~\%. Aggregating over outputs as well as inputs adds 2–9 points over GraphSAGE of the same depth. Cell types are strongly heterophilic: only 3~\% of synapses connect neurons of the same type. The right → left gap of 8 points concentrates on photoreceptors and lamina neurons, whose outgoing synapses are underdetected in FlyWire (\citealp{matsliah2024}) and whose connection counts differ between the hemispheres.

The result replicates within MCNS: the GNN reaches 96.6~\% (random), 95.4~\% and 93.7~\% (hemispheres) on cell types, NTAC 7.8–9.5~\% on the hemispheres. Seeding NTAC with the predictions on which three GNNs agree yields 96.5 / 90.8~\% (FlyWire) and 97.1 / 96.2~\% (MCNS) on the unlabeled hemisphere.

A single FlyWire GNN applied to MCNS with a label-free normalization reaches 73–75~\% on cell types; the best alignment, found only post hoc, 85.7~\%. The label-free pipeline chooses the alignment and normalization by type consistency, which ranks the 30 candidates like their accuracy (Spearman 0.95–0.97), and prior matching corrects the type sizes: 87.6~\% without and 91.2~\% with prior matching, and \textbf{92.6~\% after NTAC, over 394 cell types without a single MCNS label}, against 96.6~\% for a GNN trained on MCNS. Run unchanged in the reverse direction, the pipeline reaches 83.4~\% on FlyWire (72.0~\% without prior matching; 95.2~\% trained within FlyWire). The larger gap is specific to FlyWire as a target: 2.2~\% of its neurons belong to types absent from MCNS's mapping, photoreceptors cause 21~\% of the errors (51~\% correct in the left hemisphere, 83~\% in the right), and Kenyon cell subtypes 15~\%.

\textbf{Where the accuracy comes from.} The headline number is mostly the optic lobe (Figure~\ref{fig:4}). 80~\% of MCNS's labeled neurons belong to optic-lobe intrinsic types; they are typed at 97.2~\% after NTAC and contribute 78.0 of the 92.6 points, while the other 20~\% are typed at 74.0~\%. Central-brain types transfer much worse (65.1~\%), mostly because of Kenyon cell subtypes (84~\% of their errors). The pattern repeats in the reverse direction (optic lobe 92.6~\%, all other neurons 60.9~\%) and on BANC (68.6~\% and 44.9~\%). Prior matching helps central-brain types most (+11, +16 and +26 points in the three runs).

\subsection{Rare types: the GNN selects, graph matching types}

Having seen one or two neurons of a type on the left hemisphere, the GNN types its right-hemisphere neurons at 75.9~\% (6,438 types of 2–4 neurons, out of 8,582). These types were defined by matching neurons across hemispheres (\citealp{schlegel2024}), so the task is close to pairing homologs. We matched the left and right neurons of 2–4-neuron types by graph matching (Section 3.3) and gave each right neuron the type of its matched left neuron (Table~\ref{tab:3}). Knowing which right neurons are rare-type is an advantage the GNN does not have, so we also restricted the GNN's votes to the same candidates and made them one-to-one.

\section{Discussion}

\textbf{Rare types: pairing beats classifying.} For types with two to four neurons, a trained classifier has seen one or two examples, while graph matching uses the wiring among all candidates at once. Within FlyWire, where these types were defined by pairing neurons across hemispheres, matching wins by 12–16 points; between animals, the transferred GNN's role is to decide which neurons to match. That division of labor held in three pre-registered tests (across sex, at a third of the source's density, and from brain to nerve cord with a new source) and against references built from morphology or curated by hand. Candidate selection remains the bottleneck: oracle candidates are 5–25 points better than the selected ones. One-to-one matching ignores that type sizes differ between animals; a looser assignment failed, because candidates piled onto a few well-connected neurons.

\textbf{Wiring and morphology.} In practice, a new reconstruction is typed from morphology first. On BANC's brain, unreviewed NBLAST matches type rare-type neurons better than wiring alone, but the two fail on different neurons. Where they agree, the type is almost always right (95~\%), which gives a cheap confidence flag for review. Where they disagree, a neuron that graph matching assigns is better typed by the matching, which uses all of the candidates' connections at once. Morphology in turn fixes the bottleneck of the wiring pipeline: NBLAST's rare-type neurons raise candidate recall from 70 to 95~\%. We see the two-stage pipeline as a second, independent signal next to morphology, not a replacement for it. In the nerve cord, raw NBLAST matches confuse sibling types and classes, and wiring carries most of the information.

\textbf{Common types.} Anonymous wiring identifies cell types without labeled partners, within a connectome and across complete connectomes. Prior matching is the most robust ingredient of label-free transfer: it helped on every complete or sparse target (+3.6 to +17.5 points), even with types missing from the target. Of the selection criteria, none is reliable alone; combining a size-based and an agreement-based signal chose the best or a near-best candidate in every later test except the partial volume.

\textbf{What does not carry over.} Transfer degrades with reconstruction density and fails for partial volumes. Both remove context the model relies on: in a sparse reconstruction, connections; in a partial volume, partners outside it and parts of cut neurons. For typing a partial volume, a model would need to be trained on correspondingly truncated graphs or combined with morphology. Add-ons tuned on development data are fragile: the two-tier fallback helped on the brain and hurt in the nerve cord.

\textbf{Where the approach is useful.} On a complete, dense target, a small hand-labeling budget with NTAC beats the transferred GNN on common types, so for these types the model's niche is the start of an annotation: typing with no labels, covering types that a small budget misses, and pointing reviewers to the neurons most likely to be wrong. Rare types are where label-free typing helps most, because a hand-labeling budget reaches them last; there the two-stage pipeline types 41–82~\% of the neurons without a label, and together with automatic morphology matches 65–73~\% on BANC. No label-free signal we tried detects an error that all seeds share, so a transferred labeling still needs a labeled spot check.

\textbf{Circularity.} Cell types were defined partly from connectivity, and cross-dataset labels (MCNS's \texttt{flywireType} and \texttt{mancType}, BANC's types, FlyWire names for hemibrain types) come from matching that uses connectivity. Agreement with them is therefore partly agreement with another connectivity-based procedure. The GNN never sees partner identities, which weakens but does not remove this concern. For common types, BANC's morphology-matched central-brain types are the cleanest reference we found, and there FAFB → BANC reaches only 32~\%; for rare types the same reference gives the same gain as the connectivity-based one.

\textbf{On pre-registration.} The first two pre-registered tests contradicted part of what development data suggested, and the third confirmed it. The fourth confirmed the core method but rejected an add-on tuned on development data, and it showed that an absolute threshold can be out of reach when the baseline is already strong; thresholds relative to an oracle are the better form. The sandbox let us diagnose and repair failures without spending a new dataset. Degraded copies of annotated connectomes are a cheap test bed for any method meant to type new reconstructions; we recommend them.

\textbf{Limitations.} The rare-type pipeline was developed on one brain pair and tested on one held-out brain and two nerve cords; every rare-type reference except BANC's morphology matches rests partly on connectivity. The morphology comparison is exploratory: it uses BANC's unreviewed NBLAST matches on a target whose labels were known, and was not repeated on MCNS; a reviewed or better-registered morphology match would be a stronger baseline. The matching uses only the edges among rare-type neurons; seeded graph matching, which would also align each candidate's connections to confidently typed common-type neurons, is the natural next step and was not tried. Single-neuron types are not predicted, and serial homologs in the nerve cord are not separated by the matching blocks. The recommended pipeline drops the fallback after the last test, so its all-neuron numbers come from runs reported in that test but not from a fresh one. The labeling-effort comparison draws hand labels at random within types, while a real team would label every type at least once. The setting is transductive within a connectome. The revised selection rule was chosen on 14 sandbox cases. Thinned copies cannot contain sub-threshold connections. Results depend on how neurons and classes are aligned across datasets.

\section{Conclusion}

Rare cell types, most of a fly connectome's types, can be typed without any target label by letting a transferred wiring-only GNN select the candidates and graph matching type them. The approach roughly doubles accuracy on a held-out brain (22 → 41~\%) and, with improved candidate selection, carries over to two nerve cords from a new source (40 → 61~\% and 72 → 82~\%), in pre-registered tests. It complements morphology: combined with automatic NBLAST matches, it types 73~\% of BANC's rare-type brain neurons, 6 points more than morphology alone. The GNN alone types common types of an unlabeled hemisphere or another complete connectome at 83–93~\%, mainly in the optic lobe. Sparser reconstructions cost accuracy and need a robust selection rule, partial volumes are out of reach for connectivity alone, and on complete targets a few percent of hand labels with NTAC type common types better. The model is best used to start an annotation, to type the rare types a labeling budget reaches last, and to direct the review.

\section{Density and selection (Section 4.5)}

\textbf{Density.} BANC's brain graph is sparse: the median neuron has 30 input synapses from other neurons in the graph and 4 input partners (FlyWire: 152 and 14). Thinned copies of FlyWire, which keep each synapse with probability p before the threshold of 5, reproduce BANC's statistics at p ≈ 0.35–0.4. Transfer between independently thinned copies isolates density from all other differences (Figure~\ref{fig:S1}).

\begin{figure}[tbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.92\linewidth]{figures/thinning.pdf}
\caption{Transfer between thinned FlyWire copies, with prior matching (test split, mean over 3 seeds). Rows: density of the source copy the GNN was trained on; ``augmented'': one GNN trained with a random density in [0.25, 1] per epoch.}\label{fig:S1}
\end{figure}

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,itemsep=2pt]

\item \textbf{Density explains about half of the drop on BANC}: the full-density GNN reaches 72.9~\% on a copy at BANC's density, against 55.7~\% on BANC.

\item \textbf{A sparser target is best served by a source of its density} (83.9 vs. 72.9~\% at p = 0.4), but accuracy falls even then (95.1 → 67.7~\% on the diagonal): a sparse reconstruction loses information.

\item \textbf{A denser target is benign with prior matching}: a GNN trained at p = 0.4 reaches 87.0~\% on the full-density copy (70.0~\% without prior matching).

\item \textbf{One GNN trained with random thinning approaches the density-matched GNNs on every target} (94.0, 90.9, 83.7, 63.7~\% against 95.1, 91.3, 83.9, 67.7~\%), without knowing the target's density.

\item On BANC itself, neither density-matched nor augmented GNNs beat the full-density GNN (51.2 and 55.0 vs. 55.7~\%, exploratory). There, the \texttt{density} variant, which lowers the threshold to 2 synapses, recovers strong connections that survive as weak ones; the thinned copies cannot show this, because FlyWire's public connections start at 5 synapses.

\end{itemize}

\section{Estimating accuracy without labels (Section 4.6)}

\textbf{Can one tell without labels how good a labeling is?} We fitted simple linear predictors of accuracy from the label-free properties of all 1,200 candidate labelings of the development runs and the sandbox, and tested them on BANC and the hemibrain. The pre-specified predictor (seed agreement and type-size divergence) missed by 12.8 points on average (median 8.4). It was accurate on complete targets with a non-collapsed labeling (FAFB → BANC cell types: 0.5 points). It failed when all seeds made the same error: on the collapsed MCNS → BANC labeling (predicted 44.5~\%, true 1.4~\%) and on hemibrain super classes with the FlyWire prior (seed agreement 0.94, 34.7~\% correct). Seed agreement does rank neurons and types where the transfer works: reviewing the 20~\% least-agreed neurons finds 77~\% of the errors on MCNS, 55~\% on FlyWire and 34~\% on BANC (random review: 20~\%), and per type it correlates with F1 (Spearman 0.54–0.66). On the hemibrain, where 80~\% of the labels are wrong, it is no better than random.
