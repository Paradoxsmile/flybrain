\begin{abstract}

New connectome reconstructions are typed by matching their neurons to an annotated reference. Most cell types are rare, with two to four neurons per brain, and methods that type neurons by their connections need labeled partners in the target. We type rare neurons without any target label in two stages. A graph neural network (GNN) that sees only anonymous wiring is trained on the reference and transferred to the target, where it selects candidate neurons; graph matching against the reference's rare-type neurons then assigns their types. In tests whose protocols were committed before any run on the held-out data, accuracy on rare-type neurons rose from 22 to 41~\% on the sparse BANC brain and, with an improved candidate rule, from 40 to 61~\% and from 72 to 82~\% in the nerve cords of BANC and of the male CNS. Wiring is a signal independent of morphology. In a pre-registered test on BANC's brain, combining it with automatic morphology matches (NBLAST) beat NBLAST alone by 2.3 points (95~\% CI 1.3–3.2), short of the 3 points we required; in the nerve cord, where these matches are weak, it gained 23 points. BANC's reference types come from reviewed NBLAST matches, which favors NBLAST in this comparison.

\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

Electron-microscopy connectomes of the adult \emph{Drosophila} central nervous system now exist for several individuals: the female FAFB/FlyWire brain (\citealp{dorkenwald2024}; \citealp{schlegel2024}), the hemibrain (\citealp{scheffer2020}), the male ventral nerve cord MANC (\citealp{takemura2024}; \citealp{marin2024}), the male CNS (MCNS; \citealp{berg2026}) and the female brain and nerve cord BANC (\citealp{bates2026}). Their value rests on cell-type annotations. New reconstructions are typed by matching their neurons to an annotated reference, by morphology and by connectivity (\citealp{schlegel2024}; \citealp{bates2026}). Most types are small: of FlyWire's 8,772 cell types, about three quarters have two to four neurons. Connectivity-based typing compares each neuron's synaptic partners by type, as in the definition of optic-lobe types (\citealp{matsliah2024}) and in NTAC (\citealp{schwartzman2026}), and therefore needs labeled partners in the target.

We type these rare neurons in a target in which no neuron is labeled, using only its anonymous wiring diagram: the directed graph of neurons and synapse counts. A classifier trained on the reference has seen one or two examples of each rare type. But these types were defined by pairing neurons across hemispheres and brains, and pairing neurons is what graph matching does without labels (\citealp{pedigo2023}). Graph matching needs to know which target neurons to pair, though. We therefore let a graph neural network (GNN), trained on the reference and transferred to the target without labels, decide which neurons to match, and graph matching decide their types (Figure~\ref{fig:1}a). Our contributions:

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*,itemsep=2pt]

\item \textbf{Graph matching types rare neurons once a transferred GNN has chosen the candidates.} Within FlyWire, on the same candidates, matching types 92~\% of the right-hemisphere neurons of 2–4-neuron types, against 80~\% for the GNN; without any label, the two stages raise these types from 79 to 86~\%. From FlyWire to the male CNS they rise from 54 to 82~\% (Section~\ref{sec:res-dev}).

\item \textbf{The gain holds on held-out connectomes, and candidate selection is the bottleneck.} In three pre-registered tests, on BANC's brain and from MANC to the nerve cords of BANC and MCNS, the two stages gained 10–21 points over the GNN alone (Section~\ref{sec:res-test}). Matching with perfectly chosen candidates would gain another 5–25 points (Section~\ref{sec:res-cand}).

\item \textbf{Wiring is an independent signal next to morphology.} It adds little where automatic morphology matches are good (BANC brain, +2.3 points in a pre-registered test that missed its 3-point rule) and a lot where they are weak (BANC cord, +23 points). BANC's reference types are partly NBLAST's own answers, which tilts the comparison toward morphology (Section~\ref{sec:res-morph}).

\end{enumerate}

We report all five pre-registered tests, including what failed in them (Table~\ref{tab:tests}): the first rule for choosing the GNN's preprocessing, a fallback for unmatched neurons, a threshold set too high, seeding the matching with morphology matches, and typing a partial volume.

\section{Related work}

\textbf{Cell typing from connectomes.} Fly connectomes have been annotated with thousands of types from morphology and connectivity (\citealp{scheffer2020}; \citealp{schlegel2024}; \citealp{matsliah2024}; \citealp{marin2024}). In the optic lobe, types were defined from each neuron's input and output vectors over types (\citealp{matsliah2024}). NTAC (\citealp{schwartzman2026}) automates this from labeled seeds (97.0~\% on 434 FlyWire types in our reimplementation, Supplement~\ref{sec:S-within}) and has an unsupervised mode. Learned approaches mostly use morphology (\citealp{troidl2024}) or combine it with connectivity (NeuNet; \citealp{liao2024}). Across datasets, types are matched by NBLAST (\citealp{costa2016}), connectivity alignment and combinations of both (\citealp{schlegel2024}; \citealp{bates2026}; \citealp{berg2026}). These typing methods read partner labels or are evaluated within one labeled graph; we know of no evaluation of a trained typing model transferred to an unlabeled connectome.

\textbf{Graph matching and label-free structure.} Graph matching finds the correspondence between the nodes of two graphs that best aligns their edges; the relaxed quadratic assignment problem is solved efficiently by Frank–Wolfe iterations (FAQ; \citealp{vogelstein2015}), and seeded graph matching fixes part of the correspondence in advance (\citealp{fishkind2019}). Bisected graph matching, which also aligns the connections between hemispheres, pairs bilaterally homologous neurons of the larval connectome from connectivity alone (\citealp{pedigo2023}). Adjacency spectral embedding with Gaussian mixtures recovers neuron groups in the larval mushroom body without labels (\citealp{priebe2017}). These methods match or cluster neuron by neuron; we use graph matching as the second stage after a transferred classifier has selected the candidates, across animals and connectomes.

\textbf{Graph learning and domain adaptation.} Neurons mostly connect to neurons of other types, a heterophilic setting in which standard message passing struggles (\citealp{zhu2020}). Aggregating separately over in- and out-neighbors (Dir-GNN; \citealp{rossi2023}) raises effective homophily; our model is Dir-SAGE (\citealp{hamilton2017}; \citealp{rossi2023}) with synapse-weighted means. For transfer without target labels, prior matching is a form of label-shift correction (\citealp{saerens2002}) computed by Sinkhorn scaling (\citealp{cuturi2013}), and it mirrors reference mapping in single-cell genomics, where batch effects separate a query from an annotated atlas. Our selection rule uses agreement between independently trained models, which tracks their error under distribution shift (\citealp{jiang2022}).

\textbf{Rare types.} The rare-type pipeline uses all FlyWire cell types with at least 2 neurons (8,658 types, 99~\% of the labeled neurons). A \textbf{rare type} has 2–4 neurons in the source, and the \textbf{rare-type neurons} of a target are its labeled neurons of such types. In the nerve cord, labels are MANC cell types (3,901 types with at least 2 neurons, 2,452 of them rare). Stage 1 on common types (Section~\ref{sec:res-common}) uses the 434 FlyWire types with at least 20 neurons and 10 super classes.

\textbf{References.} In every target, labels are coded in the source's type names and used only for evaluation. The main reference, the \textbf{alignment reference}, is each target's own typing aligned to the source's types by its connectome team. How it was made matters for what agreement with it means. MCNS maps its neurons to FlyWire and MANC types by matching that uses connectivity. BANC matched its neurons to FlyWire and MANC neurons by NBLAST proposals that were reviewed by hand, partly with connectivity (\citealp{bates2026}). On the rare-type neurons of BANC's brain, the reference type equals the type of the reviewed FlyWire match for 99.8~\% of neurons, and that match is NBLAST's top hit for 67.5~\%. BANC also has a \textbf{second reference}: the type of the morphology-matched FlyWire neuron (brain) and its curated typing (cord). For rare types the second references agree with the alignment references on 98–99.5~\% of neurons, so they check the scoring but are not independent evidence. Every reference we have was built with morphology matching, connectivity or both.

\subsection{Stage 1: a wiring-only GNN, transferred without labels}\label{sec:stage1}

Each neuron has six features: in- and out-degree, input and output synapses, and mean synapses per input and output connection (log-scaled, z-scored). A layer computes $h'_i = W_{\mathrm{self}}\, h_i + W_{\mathrm{in}}\, \mathrm{mean}_{\mathrm{in}}(h) + W_{\mathrm{out}}\, \mathrm{mean}_{\mathrm{out}}(h)$, with means over pre- and postsynaptic partners weighted by synapse count. The model never sees a partner's label, so it applies unchanged to an unlabeled graph. Three seeds are trained throughout (training details in Supplement~\ref{sec:S-details}).

A model trained on the source is applied to the target in three label-free steps:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,itemsep=2pt]

\item \textbf{Prior matching.} Cell-type sizes are stereotyped across flies, but a transferred classifier shifts neurons between neighboring types. We rescale its class probabilities so that the expected number of neurons per type matches the type distribution the same model predicts on its source, scaled to the target.

\item \textbf{Choice of preprocessing.} How the target graph is aligned to the source, how its features are normalized and how strongly prior matching is applied give 30 candidate preprocessings. The size-and-agreement rule chooses one without labels: the best mean rank of two properties, the divergence of the predicted type sizes from the prior and the agreement between the three seeds' labelings. It replaced an earlier rule after that rule failed on BANC (Section~\ref{sec:res-common}; Supplement~\ref{sec:S-sparse}).

\item \textbf{Refinement.} NTAC on the target, seeded with the neurons on which all three seeds agree.

\end{itemize}

\subsection{Stage 2: graph matching of rare types}\label{sec:stage2}

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,itemsep=2pt]

\item \textbf{Candidates} are the target neurons that stage 1 assigns to a rare type. The first tests used strict candidates (type after NTAC is rare). After the BANC brain test, the rule was improved on development data (Section~\ref{sec:res-cand}): broad candidates take a neuron if its type before or after NTAC is rare.

\item \textbf{Matching.} Stage 2 matches the source's rare-type neurons one-to-one to the candidates. It maximizes the agreement of the two log1p(synapse) adjacency matrices among the selected neurons by Frank–Wolfe on the relaxed quadratic assignment problem (FAQ; \citealp{vogelstein2015}), from the barycenter, for 30 iterations. The assignment is block-diagonal by hemisphere and aligns both ipsilateral and contralateral connections (bisected matching; \citealp{pedigo2023}). In the nerve cord a third, pooled neck block holds the ascending and descending neurons, whose side annotation follows the neck in MANC and the soma in the targets. Each block is padded to a square; candidates matched to padding are not assigned.

\item \textbf{Final labeling.} A matched candidate takes the type of its source neuron; every other neuron keeps its stage-1 type.

\end{itemize}

At the barycenter the gradient is nearly of rank one and the exact assignment solver takes 5–10 minutes, so the first step pairs the leading singular vectors by rank. A block of about 9,300 neurons per side takes 27 minutes and 4.5 GB on a CPU, the nerve-cord blocks under 3 minutes. The GNN uses PyTorch Geometric (\citealp{fey2019}), the assignment steps SciPy (\citealp{virtanen2020}).

\subsection{Evaluation and pre-registration}\label{sec:prereg}

\textbf{Metrics.} Accuracy is the share of labeled target neurons whose predicted type equals the reference type, computed over the rare-type neurons unless stated otherwise. Macro-F1, the mean F1 over the true types present, is given in Supplement~\ref{sec:S-f1}. The 95~\% intervals resample types (2,000 draws; 10,000 for the test of Section~\ref{sec:res-morph}), with the predictions fixed; they do not include the variability of the matching itself.

\textbf{Pre-registration.} For each test on a new dataset, the data, labels, candidates, selection rule, method, reported numbers and explicit expectations were committed to the repository before any model was run on it (Table~\ref{tab:tests}). The protocols allow only data-loading and memory fixes afterwards and require every deviation to be reported. Between tests, methods were changed only on degraded copies of the development connectomes, whose labels are known: synapse counts binomially thinned before the threshold, cell types removed, the volume restricted to the central brain, or a fraction of neurons cropped (each synapse of a cropped neuron kept with probability 0.1).

\section{Results}

\subsection{Graph matching types rare neurons better than the GNN}\label{sec:res-dev}

Having seen one or two neurons of a type in the left hemisphere, the GNN types its right-hemisphere neurons at 75.9~\% (mean of three seeds; majority vote 78.9~\%). These types were defined by matching neurons across hemispheres (\citealp{schlegel2024}), so the task is close to pairing homologs. We matched the left and right neurons of rare types (Section~\ref{sec:stage2}) and gave each right neuron the type of its matched left neuron (Table~\ref{tab:dev}).

On the same candidates, graph matching beats the GNN by 12 points (92.2 vs. 79.8~\%) and wins in every brain region, although it sees only the connections among about 8,000 neurons per side. The two methods combine without labels: the GNN's rare-type candidates contain 93~\% of the true rare-type neurons (84~\% precision), and matching them raises accuracy from 78.9 to 85.8~\%.

The same division of labor works across animals. From FlyWire to MCNS, stage 1 types the rare-type neurons at 54.2~\%. Its 18,479 strict candidates contain 84~\% of them; matching these to the 16,365 FlyWire neurons of rare types gives 74.5~\%, and broad candidates (92~\% recall) give 81.6~\%. The wiring does the work: making the GNN's probabilities one-to-one gains only 6 points, and starting the matching from them instead of the barycenter changes nothing (74.6~\%). The largest gains are where the GNN is weakest: ascending, descending and other neurons rise from 25 to 66~\%, central-brain neurons from 58 to 75~\%. Matching between two animals is nearly as accurate as between two hemispheres (85.3 vs. 92.2~\% with oracle candidates), so most of the remaining loss comes from candidate selection. Two caveats apply: stage 1 was developed on this pair, and MCNS's labels for these types come from cross-dataset matching that uses connectivity.

\subsection{The gain holds on held-out connectomes}\label{sec:res-test}

On the BANC brain, the protocol (committed in \texttt{0d1ee67} before either stage ran on BANC at this label set) fixed strict candidates and the size-and-agreement rule, which chose the best of the 30 preprocessings. In the nerve cords (\texttt{5119069}), the source was MANC, on which the GNNs were trained unchanged; the targets were BANC's cord (female, 31~\% of MANC's connections per neuron) and MCNS's cord (a second male). This test used the broad candidates and one change disclosed before any model output on a cord, the neck block.

The two stages beat stage 1 in all three targets. On the BANC brain, all four expectations held. \subsection{Where the errors are: candidate selection}\label{sec:res-cand}

With oracle candidates, matching is 5–25 points more accurate than with the selected ones (Table~\ref{tab:test}). On the BANC brain, stage 1 finds only 54~\% of the rare-type neurons (MCNS: 84~\%), while matching types 77~\% of those it finds correctly. Because BANC was used up for rare types after that test, we improved candidate selection on development data only. MCNS thinned to 25~\% of its synapses reproduces BANC's stage 1 (candidate recall 54.4 vs. 54.3~\%, accuracy 19.2 vs. 22.2~\%) and served as a stand-in (Table~\ref{tab:cand}).

Selecting more candidates helps. Broad candidates raise recall from 54 to 70~\% on the thinned copy and from 84 to 92~\% on full MCNS, and matching stays accurate on the added neurons (81~\%). The rule has no free parameter, and a second thinning realization agrees within 1–2 points. Three other changes did not help. Letting a source neuron match two candidates failed on development data (oracle candidates: 85.3 → 76.1~\%), because candidates piled onto a few well-connected neurons. A coarse fallback, which typed unmatched neurons with a model trained on types of at least 10 neurons, added 2.5–3.7 points over all labeled neurons on development data, but cost 0.4 and 1.2 points on the cords; we dropped it. The neck block did help: without it, the cords' rare types lost 5.6–5.7 points. The cost of the matching is borne by types of 5–19 neurons (−1.5 to −2.5 points), because a fifth to a third of the candidates belong to other types and receive a rare type.

\textbf{Exploratory comparison.} On the brain, morphology alone beats wiring alone (66.5 vs. 51.2~\%), but the two err on different neurons. Where NBLAST and the pre-registered two-stage typing agree, both are right in 95~\% of cases; where they disagree, NBLAST is right in 61~\% and the matching in 16~\%. We tried six label-free combinations (planned in \texttt{docs/\allowbreak{}finalize\_\allowbreak{}plan.md}, commit \texttt{9ecc769}, but run on a target whose labels were known). The best one, which takes the matched type where the matching assigns a FlyWire neuron and NBLAST's type elsewhere, reached 72.5~\%, 6.0 points above NBLAST alone. In the cord, NBLAST's top hit has the reviewed match's type in only 31~\% of cases, and wiring alone is twice as accurate.

\textbf{Pre-registered test.} Because the +6.0 was the best of six exploratory combinations, we fixed one further procedure, its contrast and its decision rule before running it (\texttt{docs/\allowbreak{}preregistration\_\allowbreak{}confirmatory.md}, commit \texttt{3949b10}). A confident NBLAST match, meaning a candidate whose NBLAST top hit is a rare-type FlyWire neuron in the same hemisphere and the top hit of no other candidate, is fixed as a seed (seeded graph matching; \citealp{fishkind2019}; 6,138 seeds in the brain). The matching then pairs the remaining candidates around the seeds, and each neuron takes the matched type where the matching assigned one and NBLAST's type elsewhere. The rule called the gain confirmed if it reached at least 3 points over NBLAST alone with a 95~\% interval above zero. On the brain, the combination reached 68.7~\% against 66.5~\% for NBLAST: a gain of 2.3 points (1.3–3.2), below the bar. Against the second reference the gain was 1.4 points (0.4–2.4). Wiring therefore adds to morphology on the brain, but by less than the margin we required, and the exploratory +6.0 is not confirmed. In the cord, the combination beat NBLAST by 23.2 points. BANC's labels had been seen before this test, so it is pre-registered in procedure, not on unseen data.

Seeding itself hurt. Without seeds, on the same candidates, the combination reached 70.6~\% (brain) and 65.1~\% (cord) instead of 68.7 and 53.7~\%. About 16~\% of the brain seeds and 32~\% of the cord seeds carry a wrong type, and fixing them prevents the matching from correcting them.

\textbf{The reference favors NBLAST.} BANC's types were assigned by reviewing and correcting NBLAST proposals (Section~\ref{sec:data}). NBLAST's accuracy against them is therefore largely the share of top hits that reviewers kept: it is right on 99.9~\% of the neurons where review kept its top hit and on 12.0~\% where review replaced it (Figure~\ref{fig:morph}b). Where reviewers replaced the hit, the pre-registered combination recovers the reviewed type for 39.1~\% of the neurons and the unseeded two-stage typing for 56.5~\%. Where they kept it, the combination loses 15.4 points against a reference that is NBLAST's answer by construction. Review also used connectivity, which may favor wiring where reviewers overrode NBLAST. Comparisons between wiring and morphology on BANC are therefore tilted, mostly toward morphology. A cleaner test would need a target whose types were assigned independently of NBLAST; MCNS, the other target, has no automatic morphology match to FlyWire. Over all labeled brain neurons, the pre-registered combination reached 61.0~\% against 56.4~\% for NBLAST.

\subsection{Stage 1 on its own: common types}\label{sec:res-common}

The pipeline relies on stage 1 transferring without labels. We developed and tested it first on the 434 FlyWire types with at least 20 neurons; the Supplement gives the details. On an unlabeled hemisphere, where the median neuron has no synapse to a labeled partner, methods that read partner labels collapse on cell types (1–9~\%), while the GNN keeps 87–95~\%. Applied to MCNS, stage 1 types 394 cell types at 92.6~\% without a single MCNS label, against 96.6~\% for a GNN trained on MCNS; most of this is the optic lobe (97.2~\%; other neurons 74.0~\%). On the sparse BANC brain it reached 61.7~\%, and density explains about half of the drop. The first rule for choosing the preprocessing failed there (for MCNS → BANC it chose a labeling that put 77~\% of the neurons into one type) and was replaced by the size-and-agreement rule, which chose the best or a near-best preprocessing in every later test except the partial volume. A partial volume, the hemibrain, cannot be typed from wiring: cell types reached 19.6~\% and super classes 74.4~\%, barely above predicting the most frequent class (73.1~\%). On complete targets, a few percent of hand labels propagated by NTAC type common types better; the GNN is worth about 1.3~\% hand labels.

\section{Discussion}

\textbf{Pairing beats classifying for rare types.} A classifier has seen one or two examples of a rare type, while graph matching uses the wiring among all candidates at once. Within FlyWire, on the same candidates, matching wins by 12 points; between animals, the transferred GNN's role is to decide which neurons to match. This division of labor held in three pre-registered tests: across sex, at a third of the source's density, and from brain to nerve cord with a new source. These are also the types a hand-labeling budget reaches last. Candidate selection is the bottleneck, and one-to-one matching ignores that type sizes differ between animals; a looser assignment failed, because candidates piled onto a few well-connected neurons.

\textbf{Wiring next to morphology.} In practice a new reconstruction is typed from morphology first, and our tests support a narrower claim than the exploratory analysis suggested. Wiring is an independent signal. On BANC's brain, where automatic NBLAST matches are good, it adds little: 2.3 points in the pre-registered test, which missed its 3-point rule, and up to 6 points in exploratory combinations. In BANC's cord, where these matches confuse sibling types and classes, it adds a lot (23 points). Where wiring and morphology agree, the type is almost always right (95~\%), which gives a cheap confidence flag for review, and morphology in turn fixes the wiring pipeline's bottleneck by raising candidate recall from 70 to 95~\%. Seeding the matching with hard NBLAST matches hurt; using NBLAST as a soft prior term in the matching is the natural next step. The size of the effect on BANC is uncertain in both directions, because the reference is partly NBLAST's own answer.

\textbf{Circularity and limitations.} Cell types were defined partly from connectivity, and every cross-dataset reference we used comes from matching that uses connectivity, morphology or both. Agreement with them is therefore partly agreement with another matching procedure. The GNN never sees partner identities, which weakens but does not remove this concern, and intrinsic nerve-cord neurons, whose references rest most on connectivity, gained no more than others. BANC's second references are not independent of its alignment references, and its NBLAST-based labels favor NBLAST. The rare-type pipeline was developed on one brain pair and tested on one held-out brain and two nerve cords. Single-neuron types are not predicted, and serial homologs in the nerve cord are not separated by the matching blocks. The recommended pipeline drops the fallback after the last test, so its all-neuron numbers come from runs reported in that test, not from a fresh one. The setting is transductive within a connectome. The size-and-agreement rule was chosen on 14 degraded development cases. Thinned copies cannot contain connections below the 5-synapse threshold. Results depend on how neurons and classes are aligned across datasets.

\textbf{On pre-registration} (Table~\ref{tab:tests}). The first two tests contradicted part of what development data suggested, and the third confirmed it. The fourth confirmed the core method but rejected an add-on tuned on development data, and it showed that an absolute threshold can be out of reach when the baseline is already strong; thresholds relative to the oracle are the better form. The fifth turned an exploratory +6.0 into a confirmed but smaller gain that missed its bar. Degraded copies of annotated connectomes let us diagnose and repair failures without spending a new dataset; we recommend them as a test bed for any method meant to type new reconstructions.

\section{Conclusion}

Rare cell types, most of a fly connectome's types, can be typed without any target label by letting a transferred wiring-only GNN select the candidates and graph matching type them. In pre-registered tests the approach roughly doubles accuracy on a held-out brain (22 → 41~\%) and, with broader candidate selection, carries over to two nerve cords from a new source (40 → 61~\% and 72 → 82~\%). Next to morphology, wiring is an independent signal: it adds little where automatic morphology matches are good (+2.3 points on BANC's brain, short of our pre-registered bar) and a lot where they are weak (+23 points in BANC's cord). Candidate selection is the bottleneck, sparser reconstructions cost accuracy, and partial volumes are out of reach for wiring alone. The approach is most useful for the rare types that a labeling budget reaches last.

\section{Details of stage 1}\label{sec:S-details}

\textbf{Training.} Cell types use 3 layers (300 epochs, class-weighted loss), super classes 4 layers (1,000 epochs); depth and options were chosen by validation macro-F1. With thousands of types the output layer and prior matching are computed in chunks, which reproduces the standard code where both can run.

\textbf{Alignment variants.} Connectomes differ in synapse detection and completeness. Each target gets variants that align one statistic with the source by a fixed rule: \emph{unaligned} (threshold 5, nothing aligned), \emph{density-aligned} (the threshold whose connections per neuron are closest to the source's) and \emph{count-scaled} (threshold 5, counts scaled so that synapses per neuron match the source's). For MCNS against FAFB we use Janelia's published scaling factor (0.581) for the last rule. In the nerve-cord test, self-connections and connections between two neurons that pass through the neck, which in the targets can lie in the brain, are dropped in all three graphs.

\textbf{Candidate preprocessings.} Each of the three alignment variants is combined with two feature normalizations (the source's training statistics or the target's own) and five strengths of prior matching, $\rho \in \{0, 0.25, 0.5, 0.75, 1\}$: 30 candidates.

\textbf{Prior matching.} We rescale the class probabilities with weights $v$ (Sinkhorn iterations) so that the expected number of neurons per type matches the type distribution the same model predicts on its source, scaled to the target; $\rho$ interpolates between the raw prediction and full matching ($p_k \cdot v_k^{\rho}$, renormalized). For partial volumes, a coverage prior uses the type distribution predicted for the FAFB neurons inside the covered volume, those with at least 10~\% of their synapses outside the optic-lobe neuropils; it needs only the list of covered neuropils.

\textbf{Selection rules.} The \emph{type-consistency rule}, used in the first test, picks the preprocessing whose labeling (mean over three seeds) is most self-consistent on the target: each neuron of a random half gets the label of its most similar neuron in the other half (synapses per partner type, weighted Jaccard), and agreement is chance-corrected (Cohen's κ). The \emph{size-and-agreement rule}, which replaced it after the first test and is used in all rare-type tests, takes the best mean rank of the Jensen-Shannon divergence of the predicted type sizes from the prior and the agreement between the three seeds' labelings.

\item \textbf{A sparser target is best served by a source of its density} (83.9 vs. 72.9~\% at p = 0.4), but accuracy falls even then (95.1 → 67.7~\% on the diagonal): a sparse reconstruction loses information.

\item \textbf{A denser target is benign with prior matching}: a GNN trained at p = 0.4 reaches 87.0~\% on the full-density copy (70.0~\% without prior matching).

\item \textbf{One GNN trained with random thinning approaches the density-matched GNNs on every target} (94.0, 90.9, 83.7, 63.7~\% against 95.1, 91.3, 83.9, 67.7~\%), without knowing the target's density.

\item On BANC itself, neither density-matched nor augmented GNNs beat the full-density GNN (51.2 and 55.0 vs. 55.7~\%, exploratory). There, the density-aligned variant, which lowers the threshold to 2 synapses, recovers strong connections that survive as weak ones; the thinned copies cannot show this, because FlyWire's public connections start at 5 synapses.

\end{itemize}

\section{Stage 1 next to hand labels, and estimating accuracy without labels}\label{sec:S-effort}

A team typing a new connectome would not rely on wiring alone; it can label neurons by hand and propagate the labels with NTAC. Fixed GNN seeds are the wrong way to combine both once hand labels exist, because NTAC never revises them: in the MCNS central brain, GNN-seeded NTAC gains 9 points between 1 and 50~\% hand labels, NTAC alone 27 points. The fallback variant is best at 1~\% on MCNS (94.1~\%, macro-F1 0.81) but hurts on BANC, where the transferred labels are less reliable. The rare-type pipeline was not part of this comparison.

\textbf{Can one tell without labels how good a labeling is?} We fitted simple linear predictors of accuracy from the label-free properties of all 1,200 candidate labelings of the development runs and the sandbox, and tested them on BANC and the hemibrain. The pre-specified predictor (seed agreement and type-size divergence) missed by 12.8 points on average (median 8.4). It was accurate on complete targets with a non-collapsed labeling (FAFB → BANC cell types: 0.5 points). It failed when all seeds made the same error: on the collapsed MCNS → BANC labeling (predicted 44.5~\%, true 1.4~\%) and on hemibrain super classes with the FlyWire prior (seed agreement 0.94, 34.7~\% correct). Seed agreement does rank neurons and types where the transfer works: reviewing the 20~\% least-agreed neurons finds 77~\% of the errors on MCNS, 55~\% on FlyWire and 34~\% on BANC (random review: 20~\%), and per type it correlates with F1 (Spearman 0.54–0.66). On the hemibrain, where 80~\% of the labels are wrong, it is no better than random.
